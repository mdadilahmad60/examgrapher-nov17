3D Graphing Calculator

About this calculator

Updated November 2026 · Free, runs in your browser

This 3D graphing calculator plots a surface z = f(x, y). Type a formula in x and y, then drag to rotate the surface, scroll or pinch to zoom, and use the range boxes to choose which part of the surface to see. Letters other than x and y, such as a or b, become sliders so you can watch how a parameter changes the shape. It also draws implicit surfaces such as the sphere x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9 when you type an equation that contains z.

How to enter a formula

  • Type the right-hand side only, for example x^2 + y^2. Starting with z = is also fine.
  • For a closed or implicit surface, type an equation in x, y and z, such as x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9. The plotter finds every point where both sides are equal.
  • Use * for multiplication or write sin(x)cos(y). Use ^ for powers and sqrt(), abs() or |x| for roots and absolute value.
  • Functions include sin, cos, tan, exp, ln, log, sqrt and abs. Angles are in radians.
  • Any other single letter, such as a or b, becomes a slider from −5 to 5.

Controls

  • Drag (or use the arrow keys) to rotate, scroll or pinch to zoom, and double-click to reset the view.
  • Top view looks straight down on the surface with no perspective, so the colours read like a contour map of height.
  • Detail sets the grid resolution. Use Fine for smooth curves and Fast on slower phones. Implicit surfaces use a 3D grid, so Fast is a good choice for the busiest ones.
  • For an implicit surface, z min and z max set the height of the box (default −6 to 6). Widen the ranges if part of the surface is cut off.
  • Set z min and z max to clip tall spikes so you can see the interesting part of surfaces such as 1/(x^2 + y^2).

Surfaces to try

  • Paraboloid: z = x^2 + y^2, a bowl that opens upward.
  • Saddle: z = x^2 − y^2, which curves up along one axis and down along the other.
  • Ripple: z = sin(sqrt(x^2 + y^2)), circular waves around the origin.
  • Gaussian: z = exp(−(x^2 + y^2)/4), a smooth hill.
  • Cone: z = sqrt(x^2 + y^2).
  • Monkey saddle: z = x^3 − 3xy^2, which has room for three downward slopes.

Implicit surfaces to try

  • Sphere: x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9.
  • Torus: (sqrt(x^2 + y^2) − 3)^2 + z^2 = 1, a doughnut.
  • Hyperboloid: x^2 + y^2 − z^2 = 1, an hourglass that opens up and down.
  • Gyroid: sin(x)cos(y) + sin(y)cos(z) + sin(z)cos(x) = 0, a repeating sponge-like sheet.

Reading the graph

The colour shows height: dark purple is the lowest point in view and yellow is the highest, and the colour bar at the right lists the values. The numbers on the box edges show the x and y ranges. Because the height axis is scaled to fit the box, compare heights using the numbers rather than the visual size alone.

Frequently asked questions

Can it plot a sphere or other implicit surfaces such as x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9?

Yes. Type the equation with z on either side, for example x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9. The plotter samples the box and extracts the surface with a method called marching tetrahedra. Inequalities such as x^2 + y^2 + z^2 < 9 are not supported.

Why are parts of my surface missing?

Points where the formula has no real value, such as the square root of a negative number or division by zero, are left blank. Points outside your z min and z max limits are also left blank.

Why is my surface a flat sheet or very spiky?

A very tall spike makes the rest of the surface look flat because the height is scaled to fit. Set z min and z max, or narrow the x and y ranges, to zoom into the part you care about.

Does it work on a phone?

Yes. Drag with one finger to rotate and pinch with two fingers to zoom. If the surface is slow to draw, choose Fast detail.

Are angles in degrees or radians?

Radians, so sin(x) uses x in radians, as in most calculus courses.

Can I save the picture?

Use Save PNG to download the current view as an image.
